Thinking about building or remodeling in the Redlands? It can be exciting to picture the views, outdoor living, and custom design choices, but this is not a plug-and-play area where every lot follows the same rules. In the Redlands, topography, drainage, jurisdiction, and subdivision-specific requirements can all shape what is realistic before your plans ever reach the permit stage. This guide will help you understand the key issues to check early so you can make smarter decisions and move forward with more confidence. Let’s dive in.

Why the Redlands takes extra planning

The Redlands is not a typical flat infill market. The rea is known for varied topography, scenic vistas, and a more open, rural feel compared with many suburban neighborhoods.

That setting is a big part of the appeal, but it also means your lot may come with added design and site challenges. The area plan places special emphasis on protecting ridgeline views and limiting light pollution, especially south of Highway 340, so details like lot orientation, roofline, wall height, and exterior lighting may matter more than you expect.

Start with jurisdiction and parcel details

One of the first things to confirm is who governs the property. The Redlands is a joint planning area involving Mesa County, the City of Grand Junction, and the City of Fruita, and rules can vary depending on whether the parcel is inside city limits or in unincorporated Mesa County. Land inside Grand Junction city limits is regulated by the city, while unincorporated land is regulated by the county. In the joint urban area, the city and county share the same future land-use map, but the city interpretation is regulatory and the county interpretation is advisory.

Before you buy a lot or finalize a remodel scope, use Mesa County’s parcel worksheet and district lookup tools to verify details tied to the exact address. That lookup is designed to help surface zoning, jurisdiction, future land use, subdivision, floodplain zone, ridgelines, geological hazards, WUI classification, slopes over 25%, mudslide areas, and faults.

What to verify before design begins

A property-specific review can save you time and money. In the Redlands, these are some of the most important items to confirm early:

  • Jurisdiction and permitting authority
  • Zoning and future land use
  • Subdivision name and recorded plat notes
  • Floodplain status
  • Geological hazards or mapped faults
  • Ridgeline or slope-related development concerns
  • Utility and service-provider details
  • HOA rules or recorded covenants

Soils, slopes, and geology matter here

The Redlands has real site-condition differences from lot to lot. The USDA notes that the Redlands soil series is very deep and well drained, formed from eolian material and alluvium derived from sandstone and shale, and is typically found on fan remnants, mesas, and structural benches with slopes of 1% to 8%.

At the same time, the broader geologic setting can create more complex building conditions. The Colorado Geological Survey publication on the Redlands connects local geology to hazards including rockfall, landslide, and earthquake risk, while the Redlands neighborhood planning documents identify unstable slopes, landslide deposits, expansive soil and rock, corrosive soil and rock, and flood-related hazards as relevant concerns.

That does not mean every lot is a problem. It does mean you should avoid assuming that one foundation approach or one builder estimate will fit every property in the area.

Why geotechnical review is often worth it

The Redlands Area Plan recommends avoiding or mitigating mapped hazard areas and says every building site should have a detailed geologic and engineering investigation before design or construction. That is a strong signal to do your homework early, especially if the lot has slope, bench edges, drainage features, or visible rock exposure.

Mesa County also maintains subdivision-specific foundation requirements, including an entry for the Redlands Area. In practical terms, some lots may have foundation rules tied to the subdivision or site conditions rather than one simple countywide standard.

Floodplain and drainage should be early decisions

Flood risk is not just a riverfront issue. Mesa County states that flooding is the most common natural hazard in Colorado and notes that local risk can come from the Colorado River, the Gunnison River, snowmelt, heavy rainfall, creeks, and washes.

In the Redlands, drainage planning matters because runoff from the southwest moves toward Colorado River drainages, and washes and streams may be subject to flash flooding under the Redlands Area Plan. That is why grading, drainage, and water flow should be part of your project planning from the start, not something left for later.

When floodplain review may affect your project

If a property is in a Special Flood Hazard Area, Mesa County says it may be subject to specific development requirements. The county also notes in its Know Your Flood Risk guidance that homes with federally backed mortgages in an SFHA generally must carry flood insurance, and some properties already have elevation certificates on file.

If you want clarity on a specific property, Mesa County’s floodplain management program offers property-specific flood-risk information and site visits. For buyers evaluating land or owners considering additions, that can be an important early call.

When stormwater permits come into play

If your project disturbs one acre or more, or is part of a larger common plan of development or sale, Mesa County requires a Construction Stormwater Permit. For larger builds and some major remodels, erosion control and drainage can become permit issues along with design issues.

Permits and site plans for Redlands projects

A beautiful design still has to match the local approval process. Mesa County’s Planning Department says a Residential Site Plan is required for all new residential land use, additions and alterations to existing buildings, ADUs, and short-term rental use changes.

The City of Grand Junction also notes that building permits are required for most construction and directs applicants to Mesa County for building permits. Because the Redlands can involve overlapping planning context, it is smart to confirm requirements based on the exact parcel before you commit to plans.

Common approvals to ask about

Depending on your lot and project scope, you may need to look into:

  • Residential Site Plan review
  • Building permit requirements
  • Floodplain review
  • Stormwater permit requirements
  • Geotechnical or engineering documentation
  • Subdivision-specific foundation review

Don’t overlook covenants and plat notes

Many buyers focus on zoning and miss the private restrictions that can shape a project. Mesa County’s plat checklist states that protective covenants or restrictions must be noted on the plat or referenced by recorded book and page or reception number.

That is why HOA rules, CC&Rs, and plat notes should be reviewed for the specific parcel or subdivision documents, not assumed from a neighborhood name. Those documents may affect design elements like exterior materials, fencing, lighting, setbacks, drainage responsibilities, or architectural review steps.

Local code and contractor selection matter

Colorado does not use one statewide building code, which makes local confirmation especially important. The Colorado Office of the State Architect notes that building codes are adopted at the local level, and Mesa County currently enforces the 2024 IRC/IBC-family codes and the 2021 IECC.

Mesa County also provides building guides that can be useful for planning, including an IECC residential checklist, a solar-ready checklist, a remodel/additions/alterations checklist, and subdivision-specific foundation requirements. If you are comparing bids, those local documents can help you ask better questions.

What to confirm with your contractor

Mesa County’s permit-to-build process requires contractor license names and numbers before permit issuance. The county’s contractor guidance also recommends verifying licensing, registration, bonding, and insurance, checking references, confirming who will pull permits, obtaining signed and stamped plans when needed, and putting scope, price, schedule, and warranty terms in writing.

A practical contractor checklist includes:

  • Verify current local licensing
  • Ask who is responsible for permits
  • Confirm code cycle familiarity
  • Request references for similar projects
  • Clarify whether plans need to be signed and stamped
  • Get a written contract covering scope, schedule, price, and warranty terms

A smart Redlands planning checklist

If you are building a home or taking on a major remodel in the Redlands, start with the site before you fall in love with the floor plan. A property that looks straightforward online may need additional drainage work, engineering review, floodplain analysis, or covenant research.

Here is a simple order of operations that can help:

  1. Confirm the exact jurisdiction and parcel details.
  2. Check zoning, future land use, and subdivision information.
  3. Review floodplain status and drainage patterns.
  4. Investigate slopes, faults, geological hazards, and foundation requirements.
  5. Review covenants, HOA rules, and plat notes.
  6. Confirm site-plan and permit requirements.
  7. Vet contractors for local licensing and code familiarity.

When you approach a Redlands project this way, you are more likely to spot issues early, budget more accurately, and avoid costly redesigns.

FAQs

What should you check before building on a Redlands lot?

  • Verify jurisdiction, zoning, floodplain status, subdivision documents, geological hazards, slope conditions, and whether the parcel may have specific foundation or site-plan requirements.

Do you need a permit to remodel a home in the Redlands?

  • Mesa County says a Residential Site Plan is required for additions and alterations to existing buildings, and building permits are required for most construction, so you should confirm requirements for your exact property and project scope.

Can drainage and flood risk affect a Redlands build?

  • Yes. Mesa County notes flood risk can come from rivers, creeks, washes, snowmelt, and heavy rainfall, and Redlands planning documents warn that local washes and streams can be subject to flash flooding.

Is a geotechnical report important for a Redlands property?

  • It often is. The Redlands Area Plan says every building site should have a detailed geologic and engineering investigation before design or construction.

Do Redlands subdivisions ever have special foundation rules?

  • Yes. Mesa County publishes subdivision-specific foundation requirements, and the Redlands Area is included, which means some lots may have standards tied to the subdivision or site conditions.

Should you review HOA rules or covenants before designing a Redlands project?

  • Yes. Mesa County’s plat checklist notes that protective covenants or restrictions must be referenced on the plat, so parcel-specific document review is an important step before finalizing design plans.
