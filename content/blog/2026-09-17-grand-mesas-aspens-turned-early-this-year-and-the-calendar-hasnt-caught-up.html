See where to go before leaves fall, with local insight on timing, trails, and scenic drives. ---

If you're the kind of Grand Valley resident who waits for the air to turn crisp before driving up CO-65, this is the year that habit costs you. The mountain isn't running on the usual schedule, and most of the local calendar still assumes it is.

Mesa County posted its own fall color update in the past few days, describing the county as already draped in gold, red, and amber. That's not a September 30th kind of statement. It's a right-now statement, and it lands a solid two weeks earlier than most of us are used to circling on the calendar.

The signal everyone watches is running early

Grand Mesa's aspens don't turn on a fixed date. They turn on moisture, and this year there wasn't much of it. A dry, hot summer stressed the trees before the season even started, and drought-stressed aspens do something specific: instead of the slow multi-week fade through green to gold to orange that a wet year produces, they skip ahead. Leaves change color sooner, hold less vivid tone, and drop sooner too.

That mechanism is playing out at a statewide level right now. By late August, roughly 96 percent of Colorado was registering some level of drought on the U.S. Drought Monitor, with the deepest stress concentrated in the high country, according to Westword's fall foliage coverage. Grand Mesa sits right in that elevation band, above 10,000 feet across most of its aspen stands, which is exactly the zone forecasters expect to turn first and fastest this year.

In a normal snowpack year, the Mesa's color holds from late September into the first week of October. That's the window most locals have baked into their fall plans without thinking much about it, and it's still the window showing up in outside travel guides. Westword's statewide fall foliage roundup lists the Grand Mesa Scenic Byway's recommended drive week as September 30 through October 6, a date range built for a typical year. This isn't a typical year. Drought-driven forecasts have the high country, including the Mesa, turning in mid-to-late September instead, which means the widely published recommendation may already be describing a mountain that's past its best week by the time most people act on it.

The comfortable two-week buffer regulars count on this time of year isn't available in 2026. The mountain isn't waiting for anyone's weekend.

Why the Applefest weekend might be the wrong bet

A lot of Grand Valley households have a default plan for Grand Mesa in the fall: wait for Cedaredge Applefest, drive up for the festival, catch the color on the way through. It's a reasonable plan most years. Applefest is Cedaredge's marquee event, now in its 49th year, running October 2 through 4 with its usual mix of vendors, a car show, live music, and the 5K that closes out the weekend.

The problem is timing. If the Mesa's aspens are peaking in mid-to-late September, the byway could already be past its best color by the time Applefest opens its gates. Combining the two events into one trip has always worked because the calendar lined up. This year the calendar didn't move to match the drought, and the drought didn't wait for the calendar.

There's a smaller, easy-to-miss detail that reinforces this. The Grand Mesa Visitor Center in Cedaredge, the place with the most reliable day-to-day read on where the color actually stands, closes for the season on the last Sunday in September. That means the one spot built specifically to answer "is it still good up there" shuts its doors before Applefest weekend even arrives. If you're planning to lean on local knowledge to time this trip, that knowledge stops updating before the festival does.

Four ways to see it before it's gone

If the plan is to go this week or next rather than wait for the usual weekend, here's what the mountain actually offers, based on effort and payoff:

Route Distance What it takes What you get
Land O' Lakes Trail 0.5 miles, paved Easy walk, any fitness level A wide overlook across the lake basin, good for a short stop on a drive
Mesa Creek Trail Short loop, about 900 ft gain Moderate, family-friendly A creek-side climb through mixed aspen and pine, with trout visible in the water if you look
Crag Crest Trail 8.5 miles out and back, 1,538 ft gain A real hike, plan half a day Open meadows, alpine reservoirs, and the best sustained aspen color on the mountain from the West Trailhead
Lands End Road Scenic drive, closes seasonally No hiking required Lands End Overlook, roughly 6,000 feet above the valley floor, with sightlines that reach toward the Sneffels Range

None of these require a special trip beyond the drive most Grand Valley residents already know. The difference this year is urgency. Crag Crest in particular rewards people willing to put in the mileage, but it's also the trail most likely to look thin by early October if the drought pattern holds.

The west side has its own version of the show

Powderhorn Mountain Resort runs its own seasonal event built around this exact moment, typically called Color Weekend. In past years it's meant the Flat Top Flyer lift running through the afternoon, single scenic lift rides sold by the hour for hikers who don't want to bike down, and the Stagecoach trail open for a walk back to the base with a leashed dog. Live music and food fill in the base area while the chairlift does the climbing for you.

Powderhorn hasn't posted its 2026 Color Weekend date as of this writing, and given how far ahead of schedule the color is running, it's worth checking the resort's site directly before planning around a date that assumed a later peak. A lift-served color weekend timed for early October, built around a normal year's timeline, could land after the show has already thinned out.

What a dry year actually does to a hillside of aspen

It helps to understand what's driving the timing shift, because it changes what you should expect to see once you're up there. A tree that hasn't had enough water can't sustain chlorophyll production at its usual rate. Instead of the slow transition through the full gold-orange-red spectrum, drought-stressed leaves tend to jump straight to a duller yellow-brown, and they let go of the tree days to weeks sooner than they would in a wet year. The visual comparison people reach for is a houseplant that missed a few waterings: the edges go first, and the plant pulls back rather than putting on its usual display.

That doesn't mean the Mesa won't be worth seeing. It means the window is real but compressed, and the difference between going this weekend and going in two weeks could be the difference between full color and bare branches.

One more planning note worth building into any trip up there right now: fire restrictions took effect on Mesa County's BLM and private lands within the Grand Junction Field Office starting September 4, and they remain in place until further notice. If a trip includes a campfire at one of the Mesa Lakes campgrounds, check current status before you load the truck.

The GMUG National Forest still maintains its long-running fall color hotline for weekly updates, and it's a better real-time source than any calendar built around a normal year. This year, normal isn't what's on the mountain.
