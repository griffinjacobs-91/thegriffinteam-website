Early May in Fruita has run on the same rhythm for almost thirty years. Demo tents crowd the sidewalk outside Over the Edge Sports on Aspen Street, a street party spills into the evening, and 18 Road fills with tents before a single pedal turns over. This spring, that rhythm broke. The festival that built it didn't happen. Downtown was busy anyway, just not for the reason anyone expected.

The gap year

According to the Daily Sentinel, the Fruita Fat Tire Festival started in April 1996, built by Troy Rarick and Over the Edge Sports to put a spotlight on trails that were still new and mostly unknown outside the valley. Thirty years later, it's one of the reasons Fruita has a national reputation among mountain bikers at all. In February, the festival's own website posted a short note instead of a registration link. The Daily Sentinel quoted the organizers saying they planned to "gather ourselves and try to come up with some new ideas and hopefully come back in 2027." No festival in 2026. First time in the event's history.

For a shop that built its identity around that one weekend, skipping it is a real decision, not an oversight. George Gatseos, who owns Over the Edge Sports, told the Daily Sentinel there was still a plan to mark the weekend with something smaller: a one-day event on Saturday, May 2, meant to keep a spotlight on mountain biking without trying to rebuild the full three-day production. That's a shop choosing to scale down on purpose rather than let the date pass unmarked.

Two different events in the same early-season window

What's interesting is that Fruita's early-season calendar included two different events, built on completely different models.

Over the Edge's own answer stayed small and local: a single day, organized by the same shop that's run the trails conversation here for decades, aimed at keeping mountain biking visible on Aspen Street without the multi-day infrastructure.

Separately, an event from outside the valley happened to land in the same early-season window. Gemini Adventures, an endurance-events producer founded in 2003 that's built a reputation across the Southwest for backcountry racing, runs Adventure Fest at 18 Road during the season's opening week, when riders and runners get first crack at the trails in pristine early-season shape. It's a different kind of event by design: the Sarlacc Attack 50K mountain bike race climbs 3,400 feet through The Edge, Cicada, and Sarlacc trails; the Dirty Edge Gravel 50K covers 2,700 feet of climbing through the famous 18 Road area; and the Fruita Trail Half Marathon is part of the UTMB World Series, the same international trail-running circuit that includes races most Fruita residents have watched on a screen, not stood next to at a packet pickup table. A women's gravel clinic led by professional riders Lauren Costantini and Reese Ruland rounded out the weekend, along with kids' races that start with a balance-bike heat for riders four and under.

The two events don't compete for the same weekend or the same crowd, but together they show the range of activity in Fruita's early-season calendar.

If you live near Aspen Street, that difference matters more than it looks. A shop-run day means local traffic, local parking pressure, and a familiar crowd. A UTMB-affiliated weekend means out-of-state plates, a reserved campground, and a different kind of foot traffic downtown entirely.

Two Aspen Avenue openings before the 2026 festival pause

Two storefronts on Aspen Avenue opened before the 2026 Fat Tire Festival was canceled.

Reckless Brewing Company got there first, taking over the former Sud's Brothers Brewery building on Aspen Avenue around the end of 2024. Owner and chef Russell Klase III expanded the concept from his original Reckless Roadhouse in De Beque, a saloon and smokehouse he'd run for three years in a town of about 500 people before deciding the model could work in a bigger market. Reckless is a year-round downtown business, with a seasonal menu and beer program.

A few months after that, Desierto Rojo opened on May 2, 2025, in the space that used to hold Mike's Famous Chicken, timed deliberately to land on the opening night of that year's Fat Tire Festival. Owners Jeff and Marlen Orehek knew exactly what they were doing. Jeff told the Business Times he expected the festival crowd to "get rocked" through the doors that weekend, and it did.

The rest of the year didn't move

While the spring calendar reshuffled, everything else downtown kept its usual shape. The Mike the Headless Chicken Festival, a Western Colorado tradition since 1999 built around the town's most famous resident, still runs every June. Rimrock Rodeo still fills summer Tuesday evenings. And the Fruita Fall Festival is already on the books for this year:

  • September 23 through 27, 2026, in downtown Fruita
  • Over 100 vendors expected, according to the City of Fruita's own event calendar
  • Named subevents include the Outhouse Race, a Pet Show, a Brew Fest, a Baking and Canning Contest, a History Fair, and the parade that closes out the weekend

Rim Rock Run follows in November, the last big organized event before the calendar quiets down for winter.

None of that changed. The full Fat Tire Festival did not take place this year; instead, the calendar included a scaled-down local event and a separate race weekend in the same early-season window.

What this actually tells you

If you've lived here a while, the easy assumption is that a missing festival means a quieter town. That's not what happened. What happened is that one homegrown, shop-run tradition stepped back for a year, and the same early-season window ended up carrying a scaled-down local event alongside a nationally affiliated race weekend most residents had never had reason to notice before.

That's worth knowing if you're the one living two blocks off Aspen Street wondering why May felt different this year, or why a UTMB-branded race suddenly had gravel parking reserved at 18 Road. Fruita's outdoor calendar isn't shrinking. It's just being run by more hands than it used to be, and the difference shows up in who's camping where, which storefronts feel busy on which weekend, and what kind of crowd you'll actually run into downtown depending on the month.
